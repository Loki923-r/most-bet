How the peg pyramid works

The board is built like a triangle of staggered pegs, with each lower row wider than the one above it. When the ball hits a peg, it has a roughly even chance of moving left or right, and it repeats that split on every row it crosses. At the bottom, the ball lands in one of the pockets, with the number of pockets matching the row count plus one.

Because each path is made of many simple left-or-right turns, most balls land near the centre and only a few reach the outer edges. In maths terms, the setup is a Galton board shaped by a binomial curve. The payout table reverses that curve: common middle pockets pay less, while rare edge pockets pay much more.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket value is based on the odds of reaching it, with a small house margin built in. When all pocket chances are multiplied by their payouts, the total comes in slightly below one, which is where the operator earns its edge. Most versions of the game sit in a return range close to 97% to 99%, depending on the studio and the settings selected.

A useful reminder: changing rows or risk does not improve the long-term return. These controls only reshape variance, which is the size of the swings, not the average result over time.

Licensed versions use a random number generator to decide each turn of the ball, and provably fair systems may also publish seeds so players can check results later. What no version can do is let you steer the ball once it starts moving.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the multiplier for the final drop.
Rows
The number of peg lines the ball crosses; more rows mean more pockets and sharper odds.
Risk level
A preset that changes how payouts are spread across the board without changing the basic return much.
House margin
The small gap between fair odds and paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A system using published hashes so players can check that a result was fixed in advance.
Variance
The size of the ups and downs around the average result, which is what the risk setting really controls.

Straight answers to five questions

Is every drop genuinely random?

On licensed platforms, yes. Each turn is driven by an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you verify the result afterwards.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. More rows add pockets and widen the odds structure, so edge prizes become larger while harder to hit. The overall return stays broadly the same.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

No setting is mathematically superior. Low risk keeps the swings smaller, while high risk puts more value into the edge pockets. The right choice depends on your budget and your tolerance for variance.
